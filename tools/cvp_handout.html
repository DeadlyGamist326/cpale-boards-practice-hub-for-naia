Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) Analysis

CVP analysis shows how changes in volume, selling price, variable cost, fixed cost and sales mix affect profit. Management uses it to plan profit, set prices, choose a product mix and decide how to use capacity.

1. Assumptions

  • Revenue and costs move in a straight line within the relevant range.
  • Every cost can be classified as either variable or fixed.
  • Volume is the only thing that changes costs.
  • Everything produced is sold (no change in inventory).
  • With two or more products, the sales mix stays constant.

2. Contribution margin (CM)

Contribution margin is what is left of sales after variable costs. It first covers fixed costs; whatever remains is profit.

CM per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit
CM ratio = CM per unit ÷ Selling price = Total CM ÷ Total sales
Variable cost ratio = 100% − CM ratio

3. Break-even point (BEP)

At break-even, the company earns no profit and suffers no loss: Sales = Variable costs + Fixed costs. Knowing this point helps when deciding to add a product line, change prices or enter a new market.

BEP in units = Fixed costs ÷ CM per unit
BEP in pesos = Fixed costs ÷ CM ratio

You can also solve it with the equation method: Sales = VC + FC, using unit price and unit variable cost. On a CVP graph, break-even is where the total-sales line crosses the total-cost line.

4. Target profit

To earn a target profit, CM must cover the fixed costs plus the profit.

Units for target profit = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM per unit
Pesos for target profit = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM ratio
Equation method Sales = Variable costs + Fixed costs + Target profit
After-tax target? Convert it to before tax first:
Profit before tax = Profit after tax ÷ (100% − Tax rate)
Example: ₱90,000 after tax at 25% → ₱90,000 ÷ 75% = ₱120,000 before tax.

Target profit as a percentage of sales

Peso sales = Fixed costs ÷ (CM ratio − Return on sales)
Units = Fixed costs ÷ (CM per unit − Return on sales × Price)

If the return on sales is after tax, gross it up first: after-tax rate ÷ (100% − tax rate).

5. Margin of safety (MOS)

The margin of safety is how far sales can drop before the company starts losing money.

Margin of safety = Actual (or budgeted) sales − BEP sales
MOS ratio = Margin of safety ÷ Actual sales
Profit = Margin of safety (in pesos) × CM ratio

Only sales above break-even create profit, which is why the last formula works.

6. Sales mix (two or more products)

Sales mix is the proportion in which a company sells its products. It matters because products usually have very different contribution margins.

Weighted-average CM per unit = Σ (CM per unit × % of units sold)
Weighted-average CM ratio = Σ (CM ratio × % of peso sales) = Total CM ÷ Total sales
BEP in units (total) = Fixed costs ÷ Weighted-average CM per unit
BEP in pesos = Fixed costs ÷ Weighted-average CM ratio
Package method (faster for board problems). Treat one "package" as the sales mix. For a 2:3 mix, one package = 2 units of A + 3 units of B.
1. CM per package = 2(CM of A) + 3(CM of B)
2. Packages needed = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM per package
3. Units of each product = Packages × its number in the mix
  • At any total volume, profit is higher if more of the high-CM products are sold.
  • Shifting toward high-margin products can raise profit even if total units fall. Shifting toward low-margin products can lower profit even if total units rise.
  • Units-based BEP works well for a few products. For many products, compute BEP in pesos using the weighted-average CM ratio.

7. Degree of operating leverage (DOL)

DOL measures how sensitive profit is to a change in sales. A company with high fixed costs has high operating leverage.

DOL = Contribution margin ÷ Profit before tax
% change in profit = % change in sales × DOL
Shortcut DOL = 1 ÷ MOS ratio

High leverage helps when sales are rising, since profit grows faster than sales. It hurts when sales fall, since profit drops just as quickly. A high-fixed-cost structure is not bad in itself, but it needs careful use.

8. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is a "what if" tool. It asks what happens to profit if price, variable cost, fixed cost or volume changes. Recompute the CM and profit under the new figures and compare.

Adding a fixed cost but keeping profit the same?
Required increase in sales = Added fixed cost ÷ CM ratio

9. Indifference point

When choosing between two cost structures (for example, a manual line vs. an automated line), the indifference point is the volume where both cost the same.

Indifference point = Difference in fixed costs ÷ Difference in variable cost per unit

Below that volume, choose the option with lower fixed costs. Above it, choose the option with lower variable cost per unit.
