Cost Concepts and Cost Behavior

Before CVP, budgeting or costing systems make sense, you need to know what kind of cost you are looking at and how it moves when activity changes.

1. The three manufacturing costs

CostWhat it isIndirect version (goes to overhead)
Direct materialsRaw materials that become part of the product and can be traced to it easilyIndirect materials: glue, thread, lubricants. Too small or too hard to trace.
Direct laborWages of workers who physically turn materials into finished goodsIndirect labor: supervisors, maintenance crew, factory guards
Manufacturing overheadEvery other factory costIndirect materials and labor, factory depreciation, rent, insurance, utilities, taxes and repairs
Quick test: Is it inside the factory? If no, it is a period cost. If yes, is it direct materials or direct labor? If no, it is overhead.

2. Cost combinations

Prime cost = Direct materials + Direct labor
Conversion cost = Direct labor + Manufacturing overhead
Product (inventoriable) cost = Direct materials + Direct labor + Manufacturing overhead
Period cost = Selling expenses + Administrative expenses

Direct labor belongs to both prime cost and conversion cost, so prime + conversion is more than total manufacturing cost.

  • Product costs stay in inventory until the goods are sold, then become cost of goods sold.
  • Period costs are expensed in the period they happen. Examples: sales commissions, advertising, delivery expense, office rent, office depreciation, executive salaries.

3. Manufacturing inventories

  • Raw materials: materials bought but not yet put into production.
  • Work in process: units started but not yet finished.
  • Finished goods: completed units not yet sold.

A manufacturer's statements differ from a merchandiser's in two places: cost of goods sold on the income statement, and these three inventories under current assets on the balance sheet.

5. Cost terms

CostResources given up to reach a goal, such as buying goods or services
Cost behaviorHow a cost changes when its cost driver changes
Cost poolA group of similar costs collected before they are assigned (e.g., the overhead account)
Cost objectAnything a cost is assigned to: a product, a job, a process
Cost driverThe factor that causes a cost pool to change; used as the base for allocating it
ActivityAn event or task that uses up cost in making a product or giving a service

6. Cost behavior

The activity index is the measure of activity (units, machine hours, labor hours) that drives the cost. Against it, costs fall into three groups.

CostIn totalPer unit
VariableRises and falls in direct proportion to activityConstant
FixedConstantFalls as activity rises (inverse relationship)
Mixed (semi-variable)Rises with activity, but less than proportionallyFalls as activity rises, but less sharply than a pure fixed cost
  • Examples of fixed costs: property taxes, insurance, rent, supervisors' salaries, straight-line depreciation.
  • The relevant range is the activity range the company expects to work in. Within it, costs are treated as straight lines. That assumption is what makes CVP analysis work.
Mixed cost at a new volume: split the cost first.
Fixed part stays the same; Variable part = Variable cost per unit × New volume.

7. Separating mixed costs

Every method finds the cost formula y = a + bx, where y is total cost, a is fixed cost, b is variable cost per unit of activity, and x is activity.

a. High-low method

Variable rate (b) = (Cost at high − Cost at low) ÷ (High activity − Low activity)
Fixed cost (a) = Total cost at high − b × High activity

Pick the highest and lowest activity levels, not the highest and lowest costs. Check your answer by computing a at the low point too; it should be the same.

b. Scattergraph method

Plot cost (y-axis) against activity (x-axis), draw a straight line through the points by eye, and read the fixed cost where the line meets the y-axis. The slope is the variable rate.

c. Least squares (regression) method

A statistical method that finds the line of best fit by making the sum of squared distances between the points and the line as small as possible. Solve these two equations together:

Equation 1 Σy = na + bΣx
Equation 2 Σxy = aΣx + bΣx²

8. Correlation

  • Correlation analysis measures how strongly two variables move together in a straight line. On a scatter diagram, points close to a line mean high correlation; scattered points mean low or no correlation.
  • Coefficient of correlation (r) runs from −1.0 to +1.0. At −1.0 the relationship is perfectly inverse, at 0 there is no linear relationship, and at +1.0 it is perfectly direct.
  • Coefficient of determination (r²) is the share of the change in y explained by the regression line. It measures goodness of fit. The higher r², the more you can trust the cost formula.
