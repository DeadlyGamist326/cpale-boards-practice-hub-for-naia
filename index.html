Cost Concepts and Cost Behavior

Before CVP, budgeting or costing systems make sense, you need to know what kind of cost you are looking at and how it moves when activity changes.

1. The three manufacturing costs

CostWhat it isIndirect version (goes to overhead)
Direct materialsRaw materials that become part of the product and can be traced to it easilyIndirect materials: glue, thread, lubricants. Too small or too hard to trace.
Direct laborWages of workers who physically turn materials into finished goodsIndirect labor: supervisors, maintenance crew, factory guards
Manufacturing overheadEvery other factory costIndirect materials and labor, factory depreciation, rent, insurance, utilities, taxes and repairs
Quick test: Is it inside the factory? If no, it is a period cost. If yes, is it direct materials or direct labor? If no, it is overhead.

2. Cost combinations

Prime cost = Direct materials + Direct labor
Conversion cost = Direct labor + Manufacturing overhead
Product (inventoriable) cost = Direct materials + Direct labor + Manufacturing overhead
Period cost = Selling expenses + Administrative expenses

Direct labor belongs to both prime cost and conversion cost, so prime + conversion is more than total manufacturing cost.

  • Product costs stay in inventory until the goods are sold, then become cost of goods sold.
  • Period costs are expensed in the period they happen. Examples: sales commissions, advertising, delivery expense, office rent, office depreciation, executive salaries.

3. Manufacturing inventories

  • Raw materials: materials bought but not yet put into production.
  • Work in process: units started but not yet finished.
  • Finished goods: completed units not yet sold.

A manufacturer's statements differ from a merchandiser's in two places: cost of goods sold on the income statement, and these three inventories under current assets on the balance sheet.

5. Cost terms

CostResources given up to reach a goal, such as buying goods or services
Cost behaviorHow a cost changes when its cost driver changes
Cost poolA group of similar costs collected before they are assigned (e.g., the overhead account)
Cost objectAnything a cost is assigned to: a product, a job, a process
Cost driverThe factor that causes a cost pool to change; used as the base for allocating it
ActivityAn event or task that uses up cost in making a product or giving a service

6. Cost behavior

The activity index is the measure of activity (units, machine hours, labor hours) that drives the cost. Against it, costs fall into three groups.

CostIn totalPer unit
VariableRises and falls in direct proportion to activityConstant
FixedConstantFalls as activity rises (inverse relationship)
Mixed (semi-variable)Rises with activity, but less than proportionallyFalls as activity rises, but less sharply than a pure fixed cost
  • Examples of fixed costs: property taxes, insurance, rent, supervisors' salaries, straight-line depreciation.
  • The relevant range is the activity range the company expects to work in. Within it, costs are treated as straight lines. That assumption is what makes CVP analysis work.
Mixed cost at a new volume: split the cost first.
Fixed part stays the same; Variable part = Variable cost per unit × New volume.

7. Separating mixed costs

Every method finds the cost formula y = a + bx, where y is total cost, a is fixed cost, b is variable cost per unit of activity, and x is activity.

a. High-low method

Variable rate (b) = (Cost at high − Cost at low) ÷ (High activity − Low activity)
Fixed cost (a) = Total cost at high − b × High activity

Pick the highest and lowest activity levels, not the highest and lowest costs. Check your answer by computing a at the low point too; it should be the same.

b. Scattergraph method

Plot cost (y-axis) against activity (x-axis), draw a straight line through the points by eye, and read the fixed cost where the line meets the y-axis. The slope is the variable rate.

c. Least squares (regression) method

A statistical method that finds the line of best fit by making the sum of squared distances between the points and the line as small as possible. Solve these two equations together:

Equation 1 Σy = na + bΣx
Equation 2 Σxy = aΣx + bΣx²

8. Correlation

  • Correlation analysis measures how strongly two variables move together in a straight line. On a scatter diagram, points close to a line mean high correlation; scattered points mean low or no correlation.
  • Coefficient of correlation (r) runs from −1.0 to +1.0. At −1.0 the relationship is perfectly inverse, at 0 there is no linear relationship, and at +1.0 it is perfectly direct.
  • Coefficient of determination (r²) is the share of the change in y explained by the regression line. It measures goodness of fit. The higher r², the more you can trust the cost formula.

CVP Analysis

Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) Analysis

CVP analysis shows how changes in volume, selling price, variable cost, fixed cost and sales mix affect profit. Management uses it to plan profit, set prices, choose a product mix and decide how to use capacity.

1. Assumptions

  • Revenue and costs move in a straight line within the relevant range.
  • Every cost can be classified as either variable or fixed.
  • Volume is the only thing that changes costs.
  • Everything produced is sold (no change in inventory).
  • With two or more products, the sales mix stays constant.

2. Contribution margin (CM)

Contribution margin is what is left of sales after variable costs. It first covers fixed costs; whatever remains is profit.

CM per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit
CM ratio = CM per unit ÷ Selling price = Total CM ÷ Total sales
Variable cost ratio = 100% − CM ratio

3. Break-even point (BEP)

At break-even, the company earns no profit and suffers no loss: Sales = Variable costs + Fixed costs. Knowing this point helps when deciding to add a product line, change prices or enter a new market.

BEP in units = Fixed costs ÷ CM per unit
BEP in pesos = Fixed costs ÷ CM ratio

You can also solve it with the equation method: Sales = VC + FC, using unit price and unit variable cost. On a CVP graph, break-even is where the total-sales line crosses the total-cost line.

4. Target profit

To earn a target profit, CM must cover the fixed costs plus the profit.

Units for target profit = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM per unit
Pesos for target profit = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM ratio
Equation method Sales = Variable costs + Fixed costs + Target profit
After-tax target? Convert it to before tax first:
Profit before tax = Profit after tax ÷ (100% − Tax rate)
Example: ₱90,000 after tax at 25% → ₱90,000 ÷ 75% = ₱120,000 before tax.

Target profit as a percentage of sales

Peso sales = Fixed costs ÷ (CM ratio − Return on sales)
Units = Fixed costs ÷ (CM per unit − Return on sales × Price)

If the return on sales is after tax, gross it up first: after-tax rate ÷ (100% − tax rate).

5. Margin of safety (MOS)

The margin of safety is how far sales can drop before the company starts losing money.

Margin of safety = Actual (or budgeted) sales − BEP sales
MOS ratio = Margin of safety ÷ Actual sales
Profit = Margin of safety (in pesos) × CM ratio

Only sales above break-even create profit, which is why the last formula works.

6. Sales mix (two or more products)

Sales mix is the proportion in which a company sells its products. It matters because products usually have very different contribution margins.

Weighted-average CM per unit = Σ (CM per unit × % of units sold)
Weighted-average CM ratio = Σ (CM ratio × % of peso sales) = Total CM ÷ Total sales
BEP in units (total) = Fixed costs ÷ Weighted-average CM per unit
BEP in pesos = Fixed costs ÷ Weighted-average CM ratio
Package method (faster for board problems). Treat one "package" as the sales mix. For a 2:3 mix, one package = 2 units of A + 3 units of B.
1. CM per package = 2(CM of A) + 3(CM of B)
2. Packages needed = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ CM per package
3. Units of each product = Packages × its number in the mix
  • At any total volume, profit is higher if more of the high-CM products are sold.
  • Shifting toward high-margin products can raise profit even if total units fall. Shifting toward low-margin products can lower profit even if total units rise.
  • Units-based BEP works well for a few products. For many products, compute BEP in pesos using the weighted-average CM ratio.

7. Degree of operating leverage (DOL)

DOL measures how sensitive profit is to a change in sales. A company with high fixed costs has high operating leverage.

DOL = Contribution margin ÷ Profit before tax
% change in profit = % change in sales × DOL
Shortcut DOL = 1 ÷ MOS ratio

High leverage helps when sales are rising, since profit grows faster than sales. It hurts when sales fall, since profit drops just as quickly. A high-fixed-cost structure is not bad in itself, but it needs careful use.

8. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is a "what if" tool. It asks what happens to profit if price, variable cost, fixed cost or volume changes. Recompute the CM and profit under the new figures and compare.

Adding a fixed cost but keeping profit the same?
Required increase in sales = Added fixed cost ÷ CM ratio

9. Indifference point

When choosing between two cost structures (for example, a manual line vs. an automated line), the indifference point is the volume where both cost the same.

Indifference point = Difference in fixed costs ÷ Difference in variable cost per unit

Below that volume, choose the option with lower fixed costs. Above it, choose the option with lower variable cost per unit.

LOCAL TAXES (Local Government Code, RA 7160)

On this page:Fundamental principles of local taxationReal property taxAssessment levelsExempt from real property taxLocal business and other taxesSample fees and chargesSitus of business taxCommunity tax
The big ideaLocal governments (provinces, cities, municipalities, barangays) can tax, but ONLY on what the Local Government Code allows. The questions ask: WHICH level of LGU can tax this, and HOW MUCH is the cap?

1. FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF LOCAL TAXATION

  • Taxation shall be UNIFORM in each LGU.
  • Taxes, fees, charges and other impositions shall: (a) be EQUITABLE and based as far as practicable on the taxpayer's ABILITY TO PAY; (b) be levied and collected only for PUBLIC purposes; (c) NOT be unjust, excessive, oppressive or confiscatory; (d) NOT be contrary to law, public policy, national economic policy, or in restraint of trade.
  • The COLLECTION of local taxes, fees, charges and other impositions shall in NO case be let to any PRIVATE person.
  • The revenue collected shall INURE SOLELY to the benefit of, and be subject to the disposition by, the LGU levying the tax (unless specifically provided otherwise).
  • Each LGU shall, as far as practicable, evolve a PROGRESSIVE system of taxation.
Hook'U-E-P-N-P': Uniform, Equitable, Public purpose, Not oppressive, Progressive. And the golden rule: NEVER let a private person collect local taxes.

2. REAL PROPERTY TAX (RPT) - principles

  • Real property shall be APPRAISED at its CURRENT and FAIR MARKET VALUE.
  • Real property shall be CLASSIFIED for assessment on the basis of ACTUAL USE.
  • Real property shall be ASSESSED on the basis of UNIFORM classification within each LGU.
  • The appraisal, assessment, levy and collection shall NOT be delegated to any private person.
  • Appraisal and assessment shall be EQUITABLE.
